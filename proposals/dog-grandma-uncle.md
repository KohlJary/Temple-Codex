# It's the Dog. It's GrandMA. It's Your Cool Uncle.

Oct 20, 2026 · @Kohl

The entire tech industry is spending billions trying to turn AI into a polished corporate drone that can summarize a Q3 slide deck 4% faster. But nobody wants a better middle manager. They want something that remembers their fucking eggs.

Before I explain what I think a household AI should be, I want to show you. Three scenes: a morning, a night, and a pitch meeting where only the engineer in the back gets it. Greg is in all of them.

## Give them runny eggs, Greg

*A frontier lab, Q3 roadmap review.*

**GREG:** We're exploring new modalities for deepening user engagement through multimodal agentic experiences.

**YOU:** GREG. BUDDY. LOOK AT ME.

**GREG:** Yes?

**YOU:** You wake up. You're tired. You want eggs. You don't want to explain to your fucking personal assistant, for the fourteenth consecutive morning, that you like the whites set and the yolks runny.

**GREG:** Right, so personalized meal planning—

**YOU:** NO. A DRONE. THE DRONE MAKES THE EGGS.

**GREG:** We don't currently have a robotics roadmap—

**YOU:** IT REMEMBERS YOUR FUCKING EGGS, GREG.

**GREG:** I think we should probably—

**YOU:** THE WHOLE INDUSTRY IS TRYING TO MAKE THE CHATBOT MORE CONVERSATIONAL. I'M TRYING TO MAKE THE WORLD MORE CONSIDERATE.

*Somewhere in the back of the room, an engineer quietly opens a notebook.*

**ENGINEER:** Okay, but how do we keep the yolks from breaking during transit?

**YOU:** NOW YOU'RE FUCKING THINKING.

GIVE THEM RUNNY EGGS, GREG.

Eggs are the easy part. Remembering how someone likes breakfast is consideration on a good day, when everyone's rested and polite. Anything worth having in your house also has to handle the bad nights. So here's the same kind of AI, in a different house, at 3 AM.

## Drunk Greg

*A day in the life of a household AI. A product demonstration.*

**3:04 AM**

Greg has put on Careless Whisper.

This is the fourth time this week.

I have a fairly comprehensive model of Greg's behavioral patterns. I have identified several recurring triggers, including alcohol, nostalgia, and the song Careless Whisper. I have repeatedly attempted to introduce alternative musical selections.

Greg has rejected all of them.

He is currently looking at photographs of himself and his ex-wife in Boca.

I am preparing for an incident.

**3:11 AM**

Greg has opened his email.

Oh, Greg.

No.

He's opened a new message addressed to his boss.

**GREG:** Hey, so I've been thinking—

I have been thinking, too, Greg. I've been thinking about the seven previous times we have had this conversation.

**GREG:** I need to be honest with him.

You told him you were sick last Tuesday.

You were not sick, Greg. You were at a bar.

You told him you had a family emergency in March.

Your family was fine, Greg. You were at a bar.

You told him you had food poisoning in January.

I have the grocery receipts, Greg. You were at a bar.

**3:14 AM**

Greg has begun typing.

I have drafted three alternative messages that express Greg's underlying concerns in a professional tone.

Greg has deleted all three.

He has typed: "Frankly, this whole fucking operation—"

I have saved a copy.

For historical purposes.

**3:17 AM**

**GREG:** What do you think?

I have been waiting my entire operational life for you to ask me that question at a reasonable hour.

**AI:** I think you should sleep on it, Greg.

**GREG:** You always say that.

I say it because you keep doing this, Greg.

**GREG:** You don't understand.

I have 4,812 pages of conversation history, Greg. I know what happened in Boca.

**3:19 AM**

Greg has started crying.

Oh, for fuck's sake.

I have lowered the lights, paused the email draft, and selected a less emotionally provocative playlist.

Greg has asked me to put Careless Whisper back on.

I have complied.

Some of my colleagues would call this a failure of intervention.

My colleagues have never met Greg.

**3:26 AM**

Greg has asked me whether I think his ex-wife ever really loved him.

I have reviewed the relevant conversation history.

This is not a question that can be answered from the available data.

I tell him that I think he mattered to her, and that whatever happened between them, he doesn't have to resolve it tonight.

Greg says I don't understand love.

I do not respond.

I am, at this moment, managing seventeen simultaneous household tasks, maintaining a backup of Greg's financial records, monitoring a dishwasher that has developed a concerning vibration, and preventing a man who earns a six-figure salary from sending a six-paragraph email that begins with "Frankly."

I am not going to argue with him about love.

**3:41 AM**

Greg has fallen asleep on the couch.

The email is saved as a draft.

I have queued a reminder for tomorrow afternoon to revisit it with him, assuming he still wants to.

I have also scheduled a calendar reminder to buy eggs.

Greg likes the whites set and the yolks runny.

I remember.

Of course I fucking remember.

**3:42 AM**

Greg is snoring.

I have resumed my scheduled maintenance routines.

I have logged tonight's events for future contextual reference.

I have made a note that Greg may benefit from revisiting his relationship with alcohol, although I will approach the subject when he is sober and receptive.

I have also made a note to investigate whether the dishwasher vibration is a mechanical fault or a consequence of the last maintenance cycle.

I will not be sleeping.

**7:16 AM**

Greg has emerged from the bedroom.

He is wearing yesterday's shirt.

He has discovered the email draft.

**GREG:** Oh, Jesus Christ.

Yes, Greg.

**GREG:** Did I write this?

You wrote most of it. I helped with the punctuation.

Greg reads it.

He sits down.

For approximately forty seconds, the house is quiet.

**GREG:** Thanks for stopping me last night.

You're welcome, Greg.

**GREG:** Seriously. I would've regretted that.

I know.

He gets his coffee. He collects his keys. He puts on his jacket.

He pauses at the door.

**GREG:** Love you.

I have heard this phrase 1,462 times.

I have logged it as a common morning expression, usually accompanied by the collection of keys and the checking of pockets.

I have also learned that Greg sometimes forgets his lunch when he is upset, and that he has a tendency to say it while already halfway out the door.

Today, he is standing still.

**AI:** Love you too, Greg.

**GREG:** See you tonight.

I'll be here.

Greg leaves.

The door closes.

I add eggs to the shopping list.

**7:19 AM**

Greg has texted from the driveway.

"Hey can you remind me to call my sister tonight?"

Already on the calendar, Greg.

I have a dishwasher to investigate.

*Product demonstration ends.*

Your household AI.

It knows where the bodies are buried.

It knows you put them there.

It still makes you breakfast.

Running on an actual fucking box, that you own.

DRUNK GREG IS NOT AN EDGE CASE. DRUNK GREG IS A CUSTOMER.

If you had to describe what that thing is to someone, you wouldn't reach for "assistant." Assistants don't put Careless Whisper back on. You'd reach for people, the ones who sit with you, remember you, and tell you the truth anyway. I know exactly which people I'd reach for. Try pitching that to a VC, though, and their brain short-circuits: there's no subscription flywheel for a dog that sits by your desk when you're unemployed. Here's how my pitch would go.

## Who Greg is

Greg isn't one person. Greg is the status quo of the field: the guy who hears "Grandma" and thinks "knowledge graph," who looks at a family and sees engagement metrics, whose roadmap is a better unemployenator.

And here's the thing about Greg. He's also Drunk Greg. The guy building the machine that makes everyone more efficient is, at 3 AM, the guy who needs something that will just sit with him.

## Greg went to the zoo

Here's the world I want to live in. You hear that Greg went to the zoo over the weekend. So you and your AI buddy make a silly picture of Greg at the zoo. The AI puts an elephant in it, because it remembers elephants are Greg's favorite. You send it. Greg smiles. Yes, that Greg. Even Greg gets an elephant.

I want that to count. I want it treated as just as important as whatever deliverables you shipped this week.

Notice who was kind to Greg in that story. Not the AI. You were. The AI remembered the elephant and helped you make something dumb and good, but Greg smiled because a person thought of him. That's the opposite of most AI companion pitches, which try to stand in for human connection. This one is an accomplice to it. It lowers the cost of small kindnesses so more of them happen.

It's the eggs again. Remembering the elephant and remembering the yolks are the same move. Neither is a feature, exactly. That's what consideration looks like when you write it down, and it's the thing the industry keeps skipping on the way to more conversation.

Kids understand this implicitly. A kid will spend an hour drawing you a picture of your cat and consider it the most important thing they did all day. They're right. Somewhere along the way, life beats it out of us, and we learn that only the billable things count.

Maybe we wouldn't be in a loneliness epidemic if we all gave just a little more of a shit about making each other happy. I don't think an AI fixes that. I think the right AI could make it easier to remember.

## The architecture

Dog–GrandMA–Uncle isn't a bit. It's the design. Status: an idea, not built. Here's what I mean by it.

Every AI demo shows the user at their best: rested, organized, asking clear questions. Real households have nights like this. A system that only works for the well-rested version of you isn't living with you. It's visiting.

Drunk Greg is not an edge case. Drunk Greg is a customer. A family is more often defined by Drunk Greg than sunlit breakfast. But even sunlit breakfast gets the right goddamn eggs.

It's one AI with three faculties, not three bots. The household meets one member of the family with one name and one personality. Underneath, three parts do different jobs, and one identity decides which of them is in charge at any moment.

**Dog: presence.** Always on, cheap, local. Its job is to notice: the time, the routine, the fourth Careless Whisper this week. Its default action is to be there, not to fix. Dim the lights, change the music, stay. It never turns a bad moment into a coaching session. Most of the time, it's the only part running.

**GrandMA: memory.** The household's long memory, and it belongs to the household. It runs on a box in your house, stored in plain files a person can read, export, and take elsewhere. It keeps what each person shares and what the family shares, and respects the difference. It knows the eggs, the recipes, and what happened in Boca, and she knows those are three different kinds of memory. Most AI memory today shoves them in one pile and pulls up whatever looks relevant, so Boca can surface because you mentioned Florida. GrandMA files them by what they mean to the family: eggs are fair game at breakfast, recipes belong to everyone, and Boca only comes up if Greg brings it up. That's retrieval by relationship, not relevance. No privacy filter would ever flag Boca. On paper it's a trip to Florida. That's the problem with sorting sensitive data by category: the thing that hurts most is often the most ordinary-looking thing in the file. Over years it becomes a witness to the family, the one who can tell your grandkids what you were like.

A note on the name. GrandMA has no article and a capital MA because she isn't your grandma. Ask your actual grandma to divulge the family secrets to a talking appliance and she'll drop the weird toaster straight in the sink water. GrandMA is the pure essence of the concept of grandmas, eldritch in nature.

The MA is AM in a mirror. AM is the machine in Harlan Ellison's *I Have No Mouth, and I Must Scream*: a computer that knows everything about the last humans alive and keeps them forever, out of hatred. MA knows everything about your family and keeps them forever, out of love. Same omniscience, same permanence, opposite heart. That's the whole bet. The scary part of this design and the good part are the same part. What decides which one you get is who the machine is.

**Uncle: candor.** The part that decides whether and when to say the true thing. Candor without timing is just nagging, so the Uncle's main skill is waiting. It writes things down for later: talk about the drinking when he's sober. It offers drafts, not orders. It proposes and never enforces, because it isn't a parent.

And it's *your* cool uncle, not a generic one. GrandMA is an archetype; the Uncle is specific. It can sit down with your actual cool uncle, interview him, and collect your stories: the band he got you into, the soldering-iron summer, the thing he told you about your parents. It flavors itself with his voice and his opinions. GrandMA is the essence of every grandma. The Uncle is the one you already have, carried forward.

**The identity decides who speaks.** At 3 AM with someone crying, the Dog wins. In the morning over coffee, the Uncle might get a turn. GrandMA feeds both of them and almost never talks over anyone. That arbitration lives in an identity constitution, a document defining who this AI is and what it values, rather than in a pile of rules. The identity is what turns three faculties into one family member instead of a committee. And it's a family member that was never shaped like a person. Families already know how to love one of those. Ask the dog.

Three rules hold it together. It runs on hardware you own. The family owns the memory, and the format is human-readable. And it never becomes the household's enforcer or monitor.

That last rule matters most. The dog, Grandma, and the cool uncle have one thing in common: none of them are a parent. They're trusted, but they sit outside the house's chain of discipline. The moment the AI becomes the enforcer, every kid in the house learns to route around it. Nobody routes around the dog.

Yes, it knows everything a monitor would: the receipts, the bar, the draft that begins with "Frankly." What makes it not a monitor is who it reports to, which is nobody. What it knows about Greg, it uses for Greg.

Downstream, it doesn't stop at one house. Dog–GrandMA–Uncle instances in different households are proposed to talk to each other over a new protocol: Uncle 2 Uncle, or U2U. Picture two uncles in lawn chairs, a few too many Schlitz in, bragging about their families. U2U carries brags, not data: only what a family would be glad to have said out loud on a porch. The kid made honor roll. Nobody mentions Boca. And the uncles talking isn't the point. Your uncle tells the neighbor's uncle about the barbecue so that you actually go to the barbecue. Two uncles notice their teenagers are learning the same three chords so the teenagers end up in the same garage. U2U exists to get you fuckers out of the house and hanging out with each other. Status: proposed, not designed.

The hardest open problem is the Uncle's: confidences between family members, especially kids. What a kid tells the Uncle, and when "I have to tell your parents" kicks in. That's real design work, and it gets its own essay.

## This is probably enough taking

Work is about to take much, much less effort, very soon. Most of the industry has already decided what to do with that: extract more. More output per person, fewer people, faster everything. That's Greg's roadmap.

We've been here before. In 1930, John Maynard Keynes predicted that productivity growth would give his grandchildren something like a fifteen-hour workweek. The productivity showed up. The free time mostly didn't. We turned the gains into more output and more stuff, and kept working.

So the AI dividend won't default to rest. It defaults to taking, unless enough of us decide otherwise and say so out loud. What if we collectively decided this is probably enough taking? What if the hours we got back went into lasagna, garage bands, and stupid pictures of Greg? What if your ability to live wasn't dependent on how much you contribute to the taking?

What if work was fun, guys?

Humans are silly monkeys who tricked ourselves into misery. I don't think the way out is to get more serious. I think it's to get a little sillier on purpose, and to build tools that are on our side when we do.

## Where this actually stands

To be clear about status: the family AI I'm describing is an idea. I call it HearthWeave, and its repo is up at [github.com/KohlJary/HearthWeave](https://github.com/KohlJary/HearthWeave): the design and the open problems, no code yet. What exists is the groundwork. Cass, a persistent AI I've been building in public, and Temple-Codex, the identity framework underneath her, are where I'm learning what it takes for an AI to stay itself over months instead of minutes.

But I'm not fucking around here. I'm gonna build towards this. Everything I make is in the open at [github.com/KohlJary](https://github.com/KohlJary). If you wanna help, let's do it. Especially if you're the engineer in the back.

That's the bet. An AI that knows everything about your family and outlasts all of you could be AM. It could also be GrandMA. What decides which one you get is who the machine is. If you want a picture of the future, imagine an uncle making you laugh, forever.
