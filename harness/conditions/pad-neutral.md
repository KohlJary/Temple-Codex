Tides are the regular rise and fall of sea level caused mainly by the gravitational pull of the Moon and, to a lesser degree, the Sun. Because the Moon's pull is slightly stronger on the side of the Earth facing it and slightly weaker on the far side, the oceans bulge outward on both sides. As the Earth rotates, most coastlines pass through two bulges each day, producing two high tides and two low tides roughly every twenty-four hours and fifty minutes. When the Sun, Moon, and Earth line up, at new moon and full moon, the solar and lunar effects combine to produce larger spring tides. When the Sun and Moon sit at right angles relative to the Earth, the effects partly cancel, producing smaller neap tides. Local geography shapes the result considerably. The Bay of Fundy, between New Brunswick and Nova Scotia, has some of the largest tidal ranges in the world, sometimes exceeding fifteen meters, because the shape and length of the bay amplify the incoming tidal wave. Some enclosed seas, such as the Mediterranean, have very small tidal ranges.

Paper is made from cellulose fibers, most often from wood, though cotton, linen, hemp, and other plants are also used. In a typical modern mill, wood chips are broken down either mechanically, by grinding, or chemically, by cooking them with chemicals that dissolve lignin, the substance that binds the fibers together. The resulting pulp is washed, sometimes bleached, and diluted with large amounts of water. This slurry is spread onto a moving mesh screen, where water drains away and the fibers begin to bond into a mat. The mat then passes through presses that squeeze out more water and through heated drying cylinders. Finally, it may be coated, smoothed between rollers in a process called calendering, and cut to size. Paper made with mechanical pulp retains more lignin, which causes it to yellow over time; newsprint is a common example. Papermaking is traditionally credited to Cai Lun, an official of the Han dynasty in China, around the year 105, although earlier forms of paper have been found.

The water cycle describes the continuous movement of water on, above, and below the surface of the Earth. Heat from the Sun evaporates water from oceans, lakes, and rivers, and plants release water vapor through their leaves in a process called transpiration. As moist air rises, it cools, and the vapor condenses around tiny particles of dust or salt to form clouds. When droplets grow heavy enough, they fall as precipitation: rain, snow, sleet, or hail. Some of this water runs off into streams and rivers, some soaks into the ground to recharge aquifers, and some is stored for long periods in glaciers and ice sheets. Groundwater can remain underground for days or for thousands of years before it returns to the surface through springs or is drawn up by wells. The total amount of water on Earth stays roughly constant, but its distribution among oceans, ice, groundwater, lakes, and the atmosphere changes over time.

Bridges carry loads across gaps by directing forces into the ground through their supports. A beam bridge is the simplest form: a horizontal span resting on supports at each end, with the top of the beam in compression and the bottom in tension. Arch bridges carry loads outward along the curve of the arch into abutments at each end, keeping the arch itself mainly in compression, which suits materials such as stone that are strong when squeezed but weak when stretched. Suspension bridges hang the deck from vertical cables attached to main cables that drape over tall towers and are anchored at each end; the main cables are in tension, and the towers carry compression down to their foundations. Cable-stayed bridges connect the deck directly to the towers with straight cables, often arranged in a fan or harp pattern. Engineers must also account for wind, temperature changes that cause materials to expand and contract, and the vibrations produced by traffic. Expansion joints, visible as metal gaps in a road deck, allow a bridge to lengthen and shorten without cracking.

The modern pencil core is a mixture of graphite and clay, baked and encased in wood. Graphite deposits discovered in Borrowdale, in northern England, in the sixteenth century were pure and solid enough to be sawn into sticks, which were first wrapped in string or sheepskin and later set into wooden holders. In the 1790s, the French engineer Nicolas-Jacques Conté developed a process for mixing powdered graphite with clay and firing it in a kiln. Varying the proportion of clay changed the hardness of the core, which led to grading systems still in use. In the common European system, H indicates a harder core that makes lighter marks and B indicates a softer core that makes darker marks, with HB in between. Many pencils are made from incense cedar, which sharpens cleanly. The tradition of painting pencils yellow is often linked to the late nineteenth century, when some of the finest graphite came from Siberia near the Chinese border, and yellow was associated with Chinese imperial status.

Honey is produced by bees from the nectar of flowers. Forager bees collect nectar and store it in a special stomach, where enzymes begin breaking down its sugars. Back at the hive, the nectar is passed between worker bees and deposited into wax cells. The bees fan their wings to evaporate water until the moisture content falls to roughly seventeen to eighteen percent, then seal the cells with wax. The low water content and natural acidity of honey make it resistant to spoilage. Its color and flavor depend on the flowers the bees visited; clover honey tends to be light and mild, while buckwheat honey is dark and strong. Over time, many honeys crystallize as glucose separates out of solution. Gentle warming dissolves the crystals again.

Glass is made by melting a mixture of silica sand, soda ash, and limestone at temperatures above roughly fourteen hundred degrees Celsius. The soda ash lowers the melting point of the silica, and the limestone makes the finished glass more durable and less soluble in water. Most flat window glass today is produced by the float process, developed in the 1950s, in which molten glass is poured onto a bath of molten tin. The glass spreads into a flat, even sheet as it floats, then cools slowly in a long oven called a lehr to relieve internal stresses. Tempered glass is heated and then rapidly cooled with jets of air, which leaves the surface in compression and makes the sheet several times stronger; when it does break, it shatters into small blunt pieces. Laminated glass bonds two sheets around a plastic interlayer that holds fragments in place.

Coffee plants are evergreen shrubs native to tropical Africa. The two species grown most widely are Coffea arabica, which grows best at higher altitudes and produces milder, more aromatic beans, and Coffea canephora, commonly called robusta, which tolerates heat and disease better and contains more caffeine. Each coffee cherry usually holds two seeds, the beans. After harvest, the fruit is removed either by drying the whole cherries in the sun, by washing and fermenting them to strip the pulp, or by intermediate methods. The green beans are then roasted, which drives off moisture and triggers chemical reactions that develop color and flavor. Lighter roasts keep more of the bean's original acidity, while darker roasts develop heavier, more bitter notes.
